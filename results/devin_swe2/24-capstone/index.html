Oatworks

A slime-mould network foundry. You never draw a road — you design the problem (cities as oat flakes, terrain, light), and a simulated Physarum polycephalum plasmodium grows the transport network. Then you measure it like an engineer and wound it like a critic.

What you can do

  • Place oats (cities), terrain walls, and light fields that the mould avoids.
  • Watch a hundred thousand point-agents with three chemical sensors self-organise into a vein network — no brain, no plan, only sense-and-turn.
  • Analyse the living web: cities linked, network length vs a straight-line minimum spanning tree, average detour, and redundancy (loops vs. bridges).
  • Sever links — by hand or the strongest one automatically — and watch connectivity drop and heal.
  • Tune the organism's sensor geometry, deposit and decay to morph fine mesh into bold arteries.

How it works

Each agent samples the chemical field at three points ahead of it (left, centre, right), turns toward the strongest scent, steps forward and deposits a little trail. The shared trail map is diffused and evaporated every tick — faint wandering dries up, busy corridors thicken. Oats emit a chemoattractant gradient; light and terrain repel. This is Jeff Jones' 2010 multi-agent model of Physarum, which Tero et al. showed grows networks with cost, efficiency and fault tolerance comparable to the Tokyo rail system. The analysis layer thins the trail field into a skeleton, extracts a graph, and reports real metrics computed live from your dish.

Why this exists

Most Physarum web demos are pattern toys: drop attractant, admire the glow. The actually interesting claim in the literature is that a single cell does network engineering — so this tool treats the organism as a designer you can brief, and the network as infrastructure you can audit and break. Compared to city-building or traffic sims, you never lay the network yourself.

Honest limitations

  • This is a 2-D agent approximation, not a biological model. Metrics are estimates read from a rasterised trail field — a research sketchpad, not engineering advice.
  • "Cost vs MST" compares against a straight-line minimum spanning tree that ignores your terrain, so a legitimately terrain-avoiding network can look "expensive".
  • Connectivity tolerates ~4 px gaps; a cut wider than that must be genuinely re-grown, which takes real seconds — that's the organism's repair time, not a bug.
  • Nothing is stored: reloading loses the dish unless you exported JSON first.
